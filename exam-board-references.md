# The Exam Board: references and what they support

This file lists every source cited in the card table artboard (D · Card table) of *The Exam Board redesign options*. Each entry gives what the source is used for in the model and the result of checking it against the source, which was done on 16 November 2026. Journal details and DOIs come from PubMed where available.

"Fixed" means the card table text was wrong or overstated and has been corrected. "Correct" means the figure and its wording match the source.

## References

These appear in the evidence notes, setting definitions and tooltips.

### Reliability and validity

1. **van der Vleuten CPM, Schuwirth LWT (2005).** Assessing professional competence: from methods to programmes. *Medical Education* 39:309–317. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2929.2005.02094.x) · [free PDF](https://www.bradfordvts.co.uk/wp-content/onlineresources/teaching-learning/assessment-competence-capability/assessing%20professional%20competence.pdf)
   - **Used for:** reliability of 0.62 for one hour of multiple-choice testing; reliability of 0.69 for 2 hours and 0.82 for 4 hours of oral examination; 0.80 as the usual minimum for high-stakes decisions.
   - **Check:** correct. Table 1 gives multiple choice 0.62 / 0.76 / 0.93 / 0.93 and oral examination 0.50 / 0.69 / 0.82 / 0.90 for 1, 2, 4 and 8 hours. The text calls 0.80 the minimal acceptable value. The authors note that the table's figures come from single studies with different designs.
   - **Also used for (added 16 November 2026):** the key-features paper. Table 1 gives key feature case (write-in) 0.32 / 0.49 / 0.66 / 0.79 for 1, 2, 4 and 8 hours, citing Hatala & Norman 2002 (its reference 43). Checked in the free PDF. The model uses 0.324 for one hour, which reproduces all four figures.

2. **Schuwirth LWT, van der Vleuten CPM. How to design a useful test: the principles of assessment.** In Swanwick T, Forrest K, O'Brien BC (eds), *Understanding Medical Education: Evidence, Theory, and Practice*, 3rd edn. Wiley, 2019. (Copy in the project.)
   - **Used for:** the definition of validity in the validity tooltip.
   - **Check:** correct. The chapter defines validity as the extent to which the competence an assessment claims to assess is the competence actually being assessed.

### Vivas

Can-Heal. **Cited in Davis MH, Karunathilake I (2005).** The place of the oral examination in today's assessment systems. *Medical Teacher* 27:294–297. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01421590500126437) · [free PDF](https://www.res.cmb.ac.lk/medicine/indika-karunathilake/wp-content/uploads/sites/91/2019/12/Oral-examination.pdf)
   - **Used for:** reliability of 0.45 when one examiner assessed all four cases; 0.69 with a different examiner for each of four cases; 0.82 with a different examiner for each of eight cases. The viva model is fitted to these three figures.
   - **Check:** correct. The cases were role-playing orals.
   - **Note:** the project file named "AMEE guide 25.pdf" is a different paper (Shumway & Harden 2003, on assessing learning outcomes).

4. **Swing S, Bashook PG (2000).** Cited in Davis & Karunathilake (2005), above.
   - **Used for:** published reliabilities of 0.65 to 0.88 for structured oral examinations.
   - **Check:** the figure is correct. We have it second-hand only, so the card table now cites it as "in Davis & Karunathilake 2005".

5. **Jacobsohn E, Klock PA, Avidan M (2006).** Poor inter-rater reliability on mock anesthesia oral examinations. *Canadian Journal of Anaesthesia* 53:659–668. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03021624)
   - **Used for:** examiner agreement of 0.24 when examiners mark independently.
   - **Check:** fixed. The figure is correct: six experienced graders each marked videotaped mock orals on their own, and the intraclass correlation was 0.243. The card table had called the orals "unstructured", but they were modelled on the American Board of Anesthesiology exam, so that word has been removed.

### Essays

6. **Smith B, Sinclair H, Simpson J, van Teijlingen E, Bond C, Taylor R (2002).** What is the role of double-marking? Evidence from an undergraduate medical course. *Education for Primary Care* 13(4):497–503. [Record](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/what-is-the-role-of-double-marking-evidence-from-an-undergraduate/) (no DOI listed)
   - **Used for:** weak agreement between two essay markers (kappa 0.12).
   - **Check:** correct. In both years studied, the weighted kappa between the two markers of third-year community course essays at Aberdeen was 0.12.
   - **Moved (16 November 2026):** this source now supports the coursework essay, not the timed essay paper, because its essays look like coursework rather than exam answers. The timed essay paper's note now cites only Anatol & Hariharan 2009 (#7), and the share of essay error that comes from the marker is listed below as an assumption.

7. **Anatol T, Hariharan S (2009).** Reliability of the evaluation of students' answers to essay-type questions. *West Indian Medical Journal* 58:13–16. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19565993/) · [free PDF](https://www.mona.uwi.edu/fms/wimj/system/files/article_pdfs/anatol_reliability_of_the_evaluation_of_students_answers_to_essay-type_questions.pdf)
   - **Used for:** weak agreement between two essay markers (kappa 0.39).
   - **Check:** correct. The kappa between markers was 0.385, in final undergraduate surgery examinations.
   - **Also used for (added 17 November 2026):** the one-hour essay range and the marker note. In the full text, each of three papers had 8 compulsory essay questions, every script was double marked, Cronbach's alpha was 0.672, and the two markers gave the same mark 46.3% of the time but were within ±5% of each other 90.7% of the time. By Spearman–Brown, 8 questions at 0.672 is about 0.50 for 4 questions. The paper does not state the time allowed, so the per-hour figure assumes 4 questions an hour.

10. **Tarrant M, Knierim A, Hayes SK, Ware J (2006).** The frequency of item writing flaws in multiple-choice questions used in high stakes nursing assessments. *Nurse Education in Practice* 6:354–363. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nepr.2006.07.002)

13. **Appelhaus S, Werner S, Grosse P, Kämmer JE (2022).** Feedback, fairness, and validity: effects of disclosing and reusing multiple-choice questions in medical schools. *Medical Education Online* 28:2143298. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10872981.2022.2143298) · [free PDF](https://boris.unibe.ch/174630/1/Feedback_fairness_and_validity_effects_of_disclosing_and_reusing_multiple_choice_questions_in_medical_schools.pdf)

### Pass marks

14. **Cohen-Schotanus J, van der Vleuten CPM (2010).** A standard setting method with the best performing students as point of reference: practical and affordable. *Medical Teacher* 32:154–160. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3109/01421590903196979)
    - **Used for:** the definition of the Cohen method (60% of the score of the candidate at the 95th percentile, after a correction for guessing).
    - **Check:** correct. A later paper on the modified Cohen method (Taylor 2011, [link](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.3109/0142159X.2011.611192)) describes the original method in the same terms.

15. **Homer M, Darling JC (2016).** Setting standards in knowledge assessments: comparing Ebel and Cohen via Rasch. *Medical Teacher* 38:1267–1277. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0142159X.2016.1230184) · [free PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/104117/3/HomerSetting%20standards%20in%20knowledge%20assessments.pdf)
    - **Used for:** the Cohen pass mark rising by 3.5 to 4 points between two years.
    - **Check:** correct, with added context. At Leeds the indicative change was 4.0 points using the 90th percentile and 3.5 using the 95th, although Rasch linking showed the two papers were of similar standard, and the Ebel standard was almost unchanged. The card table now mentions the similar difficulty.

16. **Shulruf B, Wilkinson T, Weller J, Jones P, Poole P (2016).** Insights into the Angoff method: results from a simulation study. *BMC Medical Education* 16:134. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-016-0656-7)
    - **Used for:** about 15 Angoff judges.
    - **Check:** fixed. The study is a simulation. It found that panels of about 15 judges with mixed expertise gave the most precise cut-scores, unless the test had few items. The card table had said Angoff "needs" 15 judges; it now says this is a simulation finding.
    - **Note:** the Swanwick (2019) chapter on written assessment gives lower minimums: 10 or more judges without a discussion phase, or 6 or more with one.

### The utility model and the key-features paper (added 16 November 2026)

These were added with the home page and the key-features paper. Bibliographic details come from PubMed, and each figure was checked on 16 November 2026 against the abstract or full text named in its entry.

39. **van der Vleuten CPM (1996).** The assessment of professional competence: developments, research and practical implications. *Advances in Health Sciences Education* 1(1):41–67. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00596229)
    - **Used for:** the utility model that the game uses to combine reliability, validity, educational impact, acceptability and cost (About page and credits).
    - **Check:** the citation is correct (PubMed 24178994). PubMed has no abstract for this article, so its content was not re-checked against the text.

40. **Page G, Bordage G, Allen T (1995).** Developing key-feature problems and examinations to assess clinical decision-making skills. *Academic Medicine* 70(3):194–201. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001888-199503000-00009)
    - **Used for:** the definition of a key-features case and its answer formats.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 7873006). A key-feature problem is a clinical case scenario followed by questions that focus only on the critical steps, and answers can be written in or selected from a list.

41. **Hatala R, Norman GR (2002).** Adapting the Key Features Examination for a clinical clerkship. *Medical Education* 36(2):160–165. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.2002.01067.x)
    - **Used for:** reliability of 0.49 for a 2-hour paper of 15 cases, the source of the key-features row in van der Vleuten & Schuwirth's Table 1; about 8 cases an hour.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 11869444). 101 students sat the 2-hour examination, and Cronbach's alpha was 0.49. The abstract reports a modest correlation with other measures of knowledge and clinical performance.

42. **Norman G, Bordage G, Page G, Keane D (2006).** How specific is case specificity? *Medical Education* 40(7):618–623. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2929.2006.02511.x)
    - **Used for:** two to three questions per case (code note only).
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 16836533). In three Medical Council of Canada examinations (n = 6,342), reliability was highest with 2 and 3 items per case, and about 80% of the error variance came from items within cases.

43. **Hrynchak P, Takahashi SG, Nayer M (2014).** Key-feature questions for assessment of clinical reasoning: a literature review. *Medical Education* 48(9):870–883. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12509)
    - **Used for:** scores on key-features papers correlating only modestly with knowledge tests.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 25113114). The review reports "very modest correlations" with knowledge-based examinations and internal consistency of 0.49 to 0.95 across 21 articles.

44. **Rotthoff T, Baehring T, Dicken HD, Fahron U, Richter B, Fischer MR, Scherbaum WA (2006).** Comparison between long-menu and open-ended questions in computerized medical assessments: a randomized controlled trial. *BMC Medical Education* 6:50. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6920-6-50)
    - **Used for:** the "matched by computer" answer option: the same marks and time as written answers, about 100 seconds a question, and no marking.
    - **Check:** correct (full text, PMC1618389). With 142 fourth-year students, 73.3% of long-list answers and 73.5% of written answers were correct, and mean response times were 101 and 107 seconds (p = 0.65). Long-list answers were scored electronically. In the trial, students could also type a free answer as a backup, and these were marked by hand; the model leaves this out.

45. **Bronander KA, Lang VJ, Nixon LJ, Harrell HE, Kovach R, Hingle S, Berman N (2015).** How we developed and piloted an electronic key features examination for the internal medicine clerkship based on a US national curriculum. *Medical Teacher* 37(9):807–812. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3109/0142159X.2014.990876)
    - **Used for:** a small acceptability gain (+0.03) for a key-features paper.
    - **Check:** correct but weak (abstract, PubMed 25496712). In a pilot survey, 61 students (83.3%) agreed that the examination reflected problems seen in clinical practice, although most found it harder than a multiple-choice examination. The abstract calls development "a time-intensive process" but gives no hours. This is one survey, and the model's code labels the gain as weak.

46. **Medical Council of Canada.** *MCCQE Part I Annual Technical Report 2019.* [PDF](https://mcc.ca/media/MCCQE-Part-I-Annual-Technical-Report-2019.pdf)
    - **Used for:** the number of cases per hour in a key-features paper (code note only).
    - **Check:** correct (full text). The clinical decision-making component had 38 cases, eight of them unscored pilots, with up to three and a half hours. The report covers April 2019 to January 2020, and the PDF's metadata dates it January 2021.

47. **University of Toronto, Temerty Faculty of Medicine (2021).** Writing effective clinical decision making questions using the 'key feature' approach. Guide dated 5 October 2021. [PDF](https://meded.temertymedicine.utoronto.ca/sites/default/files/inline-files/CDM%20Guide%20Oct%2021.pdf)
    - **Used for:** two to three questions per case (code note only).
    - **Check:** correct (full text). The guide says a key-feature problem has "a case scenario, followed by 2-3 questions". It names no author, so the faculty is taken from the site that hosts it.

### Coursework essay (added 16 November 2026)

These support the coursework essay in the first-year SSC. The research behind them is in `research-ssc-essay.md`. Bibliographic details come from PubMed, and each figure was checked on 16 November 2026 against the abstract or document named in its entry.

48. **McKinstry BH, Cameron HS, Elton RA, Riley SC (2004).** Leniency and halo effects in marking undergraduate short research projects. *BMC Medical Education* 4:28. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/1472-6920-4-28)
    - **Used for:** a correlation of 0.28 between two markers of the same project reports (one of the figures behind `cwM`); supervisors marking their own students about half a grade higher, which is the basis for the acceptability penalty for single marking.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 15569395). Written reports of year-4 special study modules at Edinburgh (n = 399). Supervisors marked "approximately half a grade" higher than second markers (difference 0.42, 95% CI 0.18–0.80), and the supervisor and second marker correlated at r = 0.28.

49. **Yeo SW, Signorelli C, Vo K, Smith G (2024).** A retrospective cohort analysis comparing analytic and holistic marking rubrics in medical research education. *Journal of Medical Education and Curricular Development* 11:23821205241277337. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/23821205241277337)
    - **Used for:** correlations of 0.36 (analytic rubric) and 0.24 (holistic rubric) between two markers of the same project manuscripts. With McKinstry, this sets the model's marker agreement of 0.35 for one essay.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 39211294). Honours medical students' project manuscripts at UNSW, 2016–2021.

50. **University of Southampton (2025).** *Double-Blind Marking and Moderation Policy.* Last updated June 2025. [PDF](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~assets/doc/quality-handbook/Double-Blind%20Marking%20and%20Moderation%20Policy.pdf)
    - **Used for:** the moderation sample when an essay is marked once: all fails plus 5% of the other essays, costed as about 10% of essays.
    - **Check:** correct (full text). The policy samples "all students who have failed the component and a 5% sample". Paragraph 3.1 requires double-blind marking of any project or dissertation marked by the supervisor.

51. **Wisniewski B, Zierer K, Hattie J (2020).** The power of feedback revisited: a meta-analysis of educational feedback research. *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:3087. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087)
    - **Used for:** the evidence note for draft feedback (an effect of about half a standard deviation). The +0.08 educational impact itself is a model assumption.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 32038429). 435 studies, more than 61,000 participants, d = 0.48, with significant heterogeneity.

52. **Ghazal L, Aijaz A, Parpio Y, Tharani A, Gul RB (2018).** Feed-forward: paving ways for students' subsequent learning. *Nurse Education Today* 71:116–120. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nedt.2018.09.010)
    - **Used for:** the evidence note for draft feedback (comments on a draft gave higher scores than comments on an outline).
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 30278335). A quasi-experiment with 118 third-year nursing students in Karachi. The abstract gives no effect size.

53. **Scarfe P, Watcham K, Clarke A, Roesch E (2024).** A real-world test of artificial intelligence infiltration of a university examinations system: a "Turing Test" case study. *PLoS ONE* 19(6):e0305354. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0305354)
    - **Used for:** the reason the coursework essay carries more construct-irrelevant variance (0.40, or 0.30 when the design also has a viva), and the "Some essays were written by AI" board event. The values and the event's effects are assumptions.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, PubMed 38923941). AI-written submissions to five undergraduate psychology modules at a UK university: 94% went undetected, and they scored on average half a grade boundary higher than real students.

### AI detectors (added 16 November 2026)

These support the "AI detector" option on the coursework essay card. Entries 54 and 55 are now the 2023 baseline. Entries 56–59 are the 2025–26 evidence that replaced "no protection" with a small validity effect; the research behind them is in `research-ai-detectors-2026.md`, which lists the vendor-made studies that were excluded.

54. **Weber-Wulff D, Anohina-Naumeca A, Bjelobaba S, Foltýnek T, Guerrero-Dib J, Popoola O, Šigut P, Waddington L (2023).** Testing of detection tools for AI-generated text. *International Journal for Educational Integrity* 19:26. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-023-00146-z) · [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15666)
    - **Used for:** the evidence note for the AI detector: detection tools are neither accurate nor reliable, and disguising the text makes them worse. This is why the detector does not protect validity in the model.
    - **Check:** correct (abstract, arXiv version, which gives the journal reference). The study covers "12 publicly available tools and two commercial systems", concludes that they "are neither accurate nor reliable", finds "a main bias towards classifying the output as human-written", and reports that content obfuscation "significantly worsen[s]" their performance. The publisher's page needed a login, so the abstract was read on arXiv.

55. **Liang W, Yuksekgonul M, Mao Y, Wu E, Zou J (2023).** GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers. *Patterns* 4(7):100779. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100779)
    - **Used for:** the evidence note for the AI detector: detectors labelled most essays by non-native English speakers as written by AI. This is the basis for the acceptability penalty (−0.05), whose size is an assumption.
    - **Check:** correct (full text, PMC10382961). Seven detectors labelled 91 TOEFL essays as AI-generated with an average false-positive rate of 61.3%, while classifying 88 US eighth-grade essays accurately. A simple prompt asking ChatGPT to use more literary language cut detection of AI essays "to near zero". The authors "strongly caution against the use of GPT detectors in evaluative or educational settings". PubMed lists the article as a commentary.

56. **Van Vlasselaer M, Van Droogenbroeck F, Spruyt B (2026).** Who wrote this? Evaluating the reliability of AI detection tools in higher education. *International Journal for Educational Integrity* 22:16. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-026-00226-w)
    - **Used for:** the evidence note for the AI detector: false accusations of wholly human writing are now rare for the best tools, but the tools differ widely in what they catch.
    - **Check:** correct (full text, publisher PDF). Received 6 October 2025, accepted 4 June 2026. All four tools (Turnitin, GPTZero, Copyleaks, Pangram) classified all 40 papers by non-native English-speaking graduate students, written before ChatGPT, as human (Table 4). On 40 papers written entirely by AI, Pangram scored 26 at 80–100% and 13 at 60–80% (strict accuracy 65.0%, lenient 97.5%), while Turnitin put all 40 in its 0–20% band. Funded by the authors' faculty; the authors "have no financial or institutional connection with any of the AI-detection tools", and declare no competing interests. Added 17 November 2026 with the essay marking changes:
