Welcome to The Exam Board

In 'The Exam Board', you are tasked with designing an assessment for medical learners. The brief tells you who the candidates are and what the assessment has to measure, and it sets limits on money, staff time and testing time. You design the assessment within those limits and then send it to an exam board, which decides whether to approve it.

How the game works

  1. 1

    Choose a brief

    Pick one of the three briefs below. If you would rather describe an assessment of your own, open the sandbox instead.

  2. 2

    Build the assessment

    Add components, such as a question paper or a viva, and decide how many of the marks each one carries. After each change the game updates the utility score, which rates the whole design out of 100, and shows the evidence for that change at the foot of the page.

  3. 3

    Submit your design to the board

    A psychometrician, a student representative, a finance lead and an external examiner comment on your design, and the board gives its verdict. The game may also pick an unexpected event at random, such as a budget cut or an appeal, and show how it changes the verdict.

About

The Exam Board is a teaching simulation for people who design or run assessments in medical education. It lets you try a design and see its consequences before you use it with real candidates.

How the model scores a design

The game uses the utility model of van der Vleuten (1996), which judges an assessment on five criteria: reliability, validity, educational impact, acceptability and cost-efficiency. The model gives each criterion a score between 0 and 1.

The utility is the product of the five scores, each raised to a power set by its weight in the brief. Because the scores are multiplied, a very low score on one criterion lowers the utility however good the others are. The game shows the utility out of 100.

The four constructs

Validity depends on four constructs. A construct is a quality that an assessment sets out to measure. Each brief says how much of each construct it needs, and each component measures the constructs in different amounts. The amounts for each component are the model's own estimates.

How the board decides

A design must first meet every requirement of the brief. It must cost no more than the budget, keep within the testing time allowed, and reach the minimum reliability if the brief sets one. If it does not, the board refers it back. Otherwise the board compares its utility with the best utility that a computer search found for the same brief.

To show what the design means for candidates, the model simulates 4,000 candidates whose true competence is known and marks each of them with your design. The board reports how many would pass without being competent and how many competent candidates would fail.

The first time a design for a brief meets every requirement, the game also picks one unexpected event at random. It might be a budget cut, a shortage of examiners, leaked questions, a report of an attainment gap, essays written by AI or an appeal. In the sandbox, events happen only if you set "Unexpected events at the board" to on.

Where the numbers come from

Reliabilities, times and costs come from published studies, from the Unit Costs of Health and Social Care 2025 published by the Personal Social Services Research Unit (PSSRU), and from university pay scales and marking tariffs. Where no source was found, the model uses an estimate, and the evidence note in the game says so.

The scores are meant for teaching and for comparing one design with another. Judging a real assessment needs an analysis of its own results.

What the assessment must measure

Each row is a construct, a quality that the assessment sets out to measure. Press − or + to take away or add one point of emphasis, up to 10 points for each construct. The percentage shows each construct's points as a share of all the points, so a change to one row also changes the percentages in the other rows.

How much each criterion matters

Each criterion has a weight from 0 to 5. A higher weight gives the criterion more influence on the utility, and a weight of 0 leaves it out of the utility altogether.
